How to study with only a phone

No laptop, one small screen and a connection that comes and goes is the normal case, not a disadvantage to apologise for.

Published 7 November 20264 min readBy a student, for students

01Decide what the phone is for before you unlock it

Before the screen comes on, say the sentence out loud: I am opening this to read one chapter and answer five questions about it. A phone with no stated job becomes a phone with a job chosen for you by whatever notification arrives first.

Two settings, thirty seconds, no app needed: turn the notifications off, and put the phone face down on the table until the timer ends. If a message is important it will still be important in twenty-five minutes.

02Download once, then fly offline

Most of what you need for a chapter can be on the phone before you start. Download the book or the pages you will read, save the notes as a file, and open them once while you still have a connection. Then switch to aeroplane mode.

Offline is not a punishment. It removes the two things that cost you the most time — the notification that pulls you out, and the search that starts with a doubt and ends forty minutes later on a video about something else. A chapter you can read with the network off is a chapter you can read on a bus, in a power cut, and in a room with no signal.

03Ask one question per paragraph

Reading on a phone is harder than reading on paper because there is no page edge to hold your place. Fix that with a question. After each paragraph, type one line: what did this paragraph claim, and what does it depend on?

Saving that line is worth more than highlighting. A highlight marks where your eye went; a question marks where your understanding stopped. By the end of the chapter you have eight or ten questions, which is a revision list you can actually use at night.

04Write the answer by hand

Keep one notebook and a pen beside the phone. Whatever the screen gives you, put the answer down in your own words on paper. Two lines is enough.

The phone is where you look things up. The notebook is where you learn them.

Handwriting is slow, and the slowness is the point: it forces you to decide what the sentence is before you write it. It also gives you something at the end of a session that a scroll cannot — a page you can count.

05Keep the twenty-five minutes honest

Use a timer for twenty-five minutes, then stand up for five. In the working block, one rule: no second app. When you do not understand something, write the doubt down and keep going; do not open a search mid-paragraph.

The doubts become the next block. That is a better use of twenty-five minutes than chasing each one as it arrives, and it means the reading keeps its shape.

  1. Minute 0: state the job. Set the timer. Aeroplane on.
  2. Minutes 1 to 20: read, one question per paragraph, doubts on a corner of the page.
  3. Minutes 20 to 25: close the book, answer your own questions from memory.
  4. Break: stand, water, five minutes. Not a feed.

06What to keep on the phone

Three things, and nothing else. One folder named after the subject. Inside it, the pages you are reading this week, your own notes as a text file, and a list of questions you still cannot answer. That is a complete study kit, it fits in 20 MB, and it opens on a phone with no signal.

The folder does two useful things. It makes downloading a decision rather than a habit — you add the chapter you will actually read, not forty pages you might. And it gives every session a natural end: when you have answered the questions in the list, the session is over, and you can put the phone down without wondering whether you should be doing more.

Keep the list of unanswered questions at the top of the folder where you will see it. A doubt written down on Tuesday and answered on Thursday is learning; a doubt that disappears when the screen locks is a gap you will meet again in the exam.

07Two traps that cost the most marks

The scroll-back. A chat or a feed that answers questions instantly trains you to ask instead of think. If you notice you have asked eleven questions and written nothing, that session was entertainment with a study topic.

The photographed page. Photographing forty pages and feeling prepared is the modern version of underlining. Keep the photos if they help, but the count of pages photographed is not the count of pages known. Convert three of them into questions you can answer with the phone in your pocket.

None of this needs a laptop. It needs one job per session, one notebook, and the discipline to be bored for twenty minutes. If you want the questions and the flashcards to come from your own pages rather than someone else's, that is what this app does — and it keeps working with the network off, which is the point.

How you know it worked

You will know it worked when a forty-minute session ends with three pages of your own handwriting instead of a scroll you cannot remember.
