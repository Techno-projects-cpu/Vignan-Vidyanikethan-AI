A fixed routine for physics numericals

Most marks are lost in the first line, not the last. Seven lines, every problem, no exceptions.

Published 7 November 20264 min readBy a student, for students

01Write the given, with units

One line per quantity, with its unit, and the symbol you are going to use. If a quantity is given in a sentence — the car slows from 20 m/s to rest in 4 s — write it as three separate givens, because the sentence is hiding a zero.

Two thirds of careless errors in school physics are unit errors that were invisible in the sentence and obvious on the page. Convert everything to SI in this line, and write the conversion you did: 250 g becomes 0.25 kg, 72 km/h becomes 20 m/s. Doing it here means never doing it mid-substitution, which is where it goes wrong.

02Write what is asked, and its unit

One line: what is the unknown, and what unit will the answer be in. Then look at the given line and the asked line together and ask which relation connects them. This single question — what connects these — is the whole of numerical physics at school level, and it is skipped by almost everyone in a hurry.

If you cannot name the relation before you touch the numbers, no amount of arithmetic will save the answer.

03Draw it badly

A rough diagram, thirty seconds, no ruler. A block on a slope with the angle marked. A circuit with the cell, the two resistors and the ammeter where the question put it. A ray hitting a surface with the normal drawn as a dotted line.

Drawing badly still forces two decisions: which direction is positive, and where the boundary is. Those two decisions are what the marks depend on, and they are invisible in an equation. Also mark on the diagram which quantity you are looking for — a question mark next to the right arrow.

04Pick the relation before the numbers

Write the equation in symbols only: v = u + at, V = IR, F = ma. Then check three things before substituting:

  • Does it contain the quantity you are asked for? If not, it is the wrong relation.
  • Does it contain only one unknown? If not, you need a second relation, not more arithmetic.
  • Does it apply in these conditions? Constant acceleration, constant temperature, series connection — every school formula has one condition, and the question usually tests whether you noticed it.

05Rearrange with symbols

Make the unknown the subject of the equation while there are still letters on the page. v² = u² + 2as becomes s = (v² − u²) / 2a before a single number appears.

Two reasons. First, a symbol-only rearrangement is easy to check against the formula sheet, and a numeric one is not. Second, you will reuse the same rearrangement in three other problems this week, and doing it once with letters is doing it once instead of three times.

06Substitute, then check the size

Put the numbers in with units, and keep the units until the end. Then, before you write the answer, look at it and ask whether it is the right size for the world: a car stopping in 4 seconds travels a few metres, not four hundred; a bulb on a 5 V supply draws milliamps, not thousands of amperes; a lens with a focal length of 10 cm does not form an image 10 km away.

If the size is absurd, you have found the mistake in the line where the units stopped making sense. Go back to the given line, not to the arithmetic.

07Answer the question that was asked

Underline the answer, give it its unit, and add direction if the quantity is a vector — 2.5 m/s² down the slope, not just 2.5. Round to the number of significant figures the data justifies: if the question gave 4 s and 20 m/s, an answer of 2.333333 m/s² is claiming a precision nobody measured.

One last line, ten seconds: does the answer still answer the sentence at the top of the page? Students lose marks by solving for the speed when the question asked for the distance, and the working looks perfect.

How you know it worked

You will know it worked when a wrong answer is wrong in one place you can name, instead of everywhere at once.
