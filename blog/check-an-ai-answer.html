How to check an AI answer before you trust it

Five minutes and one book page. If an answer survives that, it is worth more than a paragraph nobody can check.

Published 7 November 20264 min readBy a student, for students

01Ask something only your page can answer

Most questions you could type into any assistant are too easy to test, because the answer is everywhere. The useful test is a question that only one page in your bag can answer: a number from a solved example, the name of a step in a diagram, the reason your teacher gave for one line of a derivation.

If the page you chose is the only place with that fact, then an answer that matches it came from somewhere real. An answer that does not match is not a small mistake — it means the answer was never reading your page at all.

Pick a fact you could only know by opening the book.

02Read the citation before you read the answer

An answer without a citation is an opinion with good grammar. Read the small line under it first — the book, the chapter, the page. Two things to check in that line before you spend any time on the words above it:

  • Does the page number exist? A chapter of thirty pages that is cited as page 91 is a warning, not a typo.
  • Does the chapter it names sound like the chapter you have? If the answer is about refraction and the citation says Light: reflection, the passage may be real but the answer may have wandered.

Only after that is the answer worth reading properly. This order matters: a confident paragraph is designed to make you stop checking.

03Open the page and find the sentence

Not the page — the sentence. Turn to the page, run your eye down it, and find the words the answer used. On a phone this is easier than it sounds: search inside the page for the one distinctive word in the quote, usually a noun you would not have chosen yourself.

When you find it, ask one more question: does that sentence actually say what the answer claims? A sentence can be true and be used to support something it never said. Current flows because the charge is pushed by the cell is not the same claim as current is the charge of the cell, and only one of them is on your page.

04The four things that usually go wrong

  1. The right page, the wrong claim. The citation is real and the sentence is not on it. This is the most common failure and the easiest to catch.
  2. The true statement that is not in your book. Everything the answer says may be correct, and it still does not help you, because the exam is set from your syllabus and your teacher will mark the wording in your book.
  3. The whole-book citation. A citation that names a file but no page cannot be checked in under a minute, so it will not be checked at all. Ask for the page instead.
  4. The smooth summary. An answer that is shorter and easier than the paragraph it came from has removed something. Compare the lengths: if the answer is half the paragraph, one of the halves is missing.

05What to do when there is no citation

Some tools will not show one. If the answer cannot name a page, treat it as a hint about where to look, not as revision. Open the index at the back of the book, find the term yourself, and read the paragraph. That is five minutes you would otherwise spend memorising a sentence that will not appear on the paper.

If a question genuinely is not covered by your book — a doubt from last week's class, a term your teacher used once — say so out loud and mark the answer as outside your syllabus. A labelled gap you can ask about beats a confident sentence you repeat in the exam.

06The five-minute drill

  1. Choose one page you have read this week and one fact on it that you have not memorised.
  2. Type a question that can only be answered from that page. Be specific enough that a wrong answer is obvious.
  3. Read the citation. If there is none, stop: that is the answer to the test.
  4. Find the sentence. Say it out loud, in your own words.
  5. Write down one place the answer was careless, even if it was right. There is almost always one.

Do this once a week and you stop being impressed by confident sentences. That is the whole skill — and it transfers to homework, to news, and to anything else that arrives with a paragraph and no page number.

This is the rule the app is built around: it answers from the pages you give it, names the page, and says plainly when your pages do not cover the question. You can try the drill there, but the drill is not the product — a book and a finger work just as well.

How you know it worked

You will know it worked when you can point at one page and one sentence, and disagree with a confident answer on purpose.
