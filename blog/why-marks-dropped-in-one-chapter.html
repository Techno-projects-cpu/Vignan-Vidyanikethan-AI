Why your marks dropped in one chapter and not another

It is almost never the chapter being harder. Find the exact line where you stopped understanding, and the rest of the chapter gets easier.

Published 7 November 20264 min readBy a student, for students

01Find the line, not the chapter

A chapter that went badly is usually described as "I don't understand chapter six". That sentence is too big to act on. The useful version is a line number: the place on page 71 where the derivation stops making sense and everything after it is noise.

Get the marked paper or the test, and put it beside the textbook. Find the first question you got wrong and cover the answer. Then read the passage that question came from, out loud, one sentence at a time, and stop at the first sentence you cannot restate in your own words. That sentence is the line. Everything before it is already yours; everything after it is where the marks went.

One sentence is easier to fix than one chapter.

02Three reasons a line stops making sense

Almost every stopping point is one of three things, and they need different repairs.

  1. A missing idea. The sentence uses a term that was defined two chapters ago and you have forgotten it. Repair: go back to the definition, write it down, come back.
  2. A missing step. The book says "therefore" and you cannot see why. Repair: find the previous line and write the missing step yourself in the margin. Textbooks skip algebra; you cannot.
  3. A missing picture. The sentence describes something in space — a force, a ray, a cell — and you have not drawn it. Repair: draw it badly, label three things, look again.

Diagnosing which of the three it is takes about two minutes and is the difference between re-reading the chapter four times and fixing the one thing that is broken.

03Check whether the chapter is actually the problem

Sometimes it is not the chapter at all, and the marks are telling you something more useful. Compare the questions you lost in this chapter with the ones you lost elsewhere:

  • You lost the easy questions in this chapter but got the hard ones elsewhere: it was revision timing, not understanding.
  • You lost the last part of every question: you are running out of time, and the chapter is where the paper happened to put long questions.
  • You lost the questions with numbers: the routine is the problem, not the theory.
  • You lost the definition and the label on the diagram: you know the idea but not the words your school wants written down.

Each of those has a different fix, and none of them is "read the chapter again". The marked paper is the most informative document you own; read it before the book.

04Fix it in one sitting, not one week

Here is the repair for a single broken line, and it takes about forty minutes:

  1. Write the line out by hand, with the sentence above and below it for context.
  2. Write the missing piece — the definition, the step, the drawing — directly under it.
  3. Close the book and explain the line to the wall in your own words.
  4. Do the two exercises in the book that use that line. Not the hard ones; the ones that use it directly.
  5. Come back in three days, and explain it again before you look at what you wrote.

If step five fails, the piece you wrote was a copy and not an understanding. That is worth knowing before the exam rather than during it.

05What this has to do with a study app

Nothing, at first. A marked paper and a pencil do this better than any software. The one place a tool helps is the paper trail: a record of which questions you lost, why, and whether the second attempt was better.

If you want that recorded rather than remembered, the progress screen in this app keeps your quiz results by topic on the device and turns the ones you missed into a revision deck. It cannot know why you lost a mark. That part is still the forty minutes above, and it is still the part that works.

How you know it worked

You will know it worked when you can point at one sentence in the chapter and say what you did not know, in one sentence.
