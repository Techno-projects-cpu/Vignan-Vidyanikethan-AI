Why writing your own questions beats re-reading

The person who writes the question has to know what the answer depends on. That is the part you are missing.

Published 7 November 20264 min readBy a student, for students

01Same paragraph, five questions

Take any paragraph from a science chapter and write five different questions from it. Not five easy ones — five that go in different directions. This is a fifteen-minute exercise that teaches more than an hour of reading, because to write a question you have to know what the answer hangs on.

Here is one paragraph from a chapter on electricity, the kind you have read four times:

Current is the rate of flow of charge. One ampere is one coulomb of charge passing a point in one second.

Five questions, in increasing difficulty:

  1. What is current the rate of?
  2. How much charge passes in one second at one ampere?
  3. If the current doubles, what happens to the charge in a minute?
  4. Why is the definition about a point and not the whole wire?
  5. A wire carries 0.5 A. How much charge passes in four minutes? What did you have to convert?

02The stems that do the work

Most students write only the first kind of question, because it is the kind the textbook's own exercises start with. Add the harder stems to your list and the chapter opens up.

StemWhat it forces you to do
What is … the rate of?Name the quantities, not the definition
What happens if … doubles?Use the relation, not the sentence
Why does it say … instead of …?Find the exact word that carries the meaning
How much … in four minutes?Convert units and calculate
Which part of this is not true for a …?Find the boundary of the rule
Where would this fail?Understand the condition it depends on

03Put in one number

A question with a number in it cannot be answered by vague recognition. Even in a theory chapter, invent one: a current, a mass, a time, a temperature. Then do the arithmetic with units, out loud.

If your invented number gives an absurd answer — a bulb using a thousand amperes, a plant growing a metre in a minute — you have found either an arithmetic slip or a misunderstanding of the scale. Both are worth the ten minutes, and neither shows up when you simply re-read.

04Ask the question that would fail you

Every chapter has one question the teacher loves and the class hates: the one that separates the students who understood from the ones who memorised. Find it. Write it in your own words, in the margin, and answer it in three lines.

If you cannot write it, look at the last two exercises in the chapter and the worked example that seemed strange. The awkward corner is usually where the question lives. Answer it once now and once in three days.

05The five-minute habit

At the end of every reading session, write three questions from what you just read. Not ten, not a set — three, in the margin, in five minutes. Then answer them from memory on the next page. That is the whole habit, and it turns reading into something you can measure.

Three has a reason. It is small enough that you will do it on a bad day, and large enough that a chapter read four times ends up with twelve questions — more than most question banks in a school book. Add the date beside each one, and after a week you can see which chapters produced questions you still cannot answer.

Nothing needs an app for this. If you do use one, use it to hold the questions and to keep the page number attached to each; the writing is the part that teaches you.

06Trade with a friend

This is the cheapest improvement available. Each of you writes five questions from the same chapter, swaps, and answers the other's questions without the book. Two things happen immediately: you meet a question written by someone whose gaps are different from yours, and you find out whether your questions were as good as you thought.

Keep the questions. Ten good questions per chapter, in your own handwriting, is a revision file worth more than any summary you can buy — because you know exactly why each one is there. If you would rather have them generated from your own pages first and then improved by hand, the quiz screen in the app does that and keeps the page number on every question.

How you know it worked

You will know it worked when a question you wrote is harder than the one in the book — and you can still answer it.
