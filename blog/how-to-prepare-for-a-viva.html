How to prepare for a viva you were not expecting

You cannot rehearse the questions. You can rehearse the three sentences every answer is built from.

Published 7 November 20263 min readBy a student, for students

01What a viva is actually testing

An oral exam is not a memory test with a person instead of paper. It is a test of whether the understanding survives being asked a second question. A written answer can be reproduced from memory; a follow-up cannot. That is the whole design, and it tells you exactly how to prepare.

So the question to rehearse is not "what is the definition of osmosis" but "why does your definition say water and not just liquid". The examiner is looking for whether the sentence has anything behind it when it is pushed.

02Prepare five sentences, not fifty facts

For each major topic, write three sentences and be able to say them without notes:

  1. The one-line version. What it is, in the book's words, in a single sentence.
  2. The why. The mechanism, in two steps, with the word "because" doing real work.
  3. The example with a number. Something you can compute or point at, so the examiner can push on the number.

Those three sentences answer most of what a viva asks, and — usefully — they are also the skeleton of a five-mark written answer. Learn them once, use them twice.

03Rehearse being pushed

This is the part everyone skips. Have someone ask "why" four times in a row about your three sentences. It feels rude and it is the best practice available. When you cannot answer the fourth "why", you have found the edge of your understanding — and that edge is exactly where the examiner will go, because it is where everybody's edge is.

If nobody will do it, do it to yourself in writing: write your sentence, then write "because…", then write "because…" again. Four times. The place it collapses is the place to read.

You do not need to know everything. You need to know where your knowledge stops, and say so before you are caught there.

04What to say when you do not know

Say it in one line, then offer the next step: I don't know that. I know that X depends on Y, so I would look at Y first. Then stop talking and let the examiner redirect. This is a strong answer — it shows a boundary and a method — and it is much better than the two alternatives: silence, and inventing something.

If you are part-way through an answer and realise it is wrong, say so. Correcting yourself out loud is what a person who understands does; examiners notice it, and it costs less than being led into an error.

05The night before and the ten minutes before

The night before: say your three sentences per topic out loud, once, no notes. Do not learn anything new; it will displace something you already know. Sleep.

In the ten minutes before: pick two topics and say their why-sentences to yourself. Do not read a chapter — reading makes you feel prepared and makes you slower to speak. Then close the book, because the first answer always comes out better when you are not holding it.

If you want a rehearsal partner that will not get tired, the viva in this app asks you to explain a sentence from your own page, pushes once when the answer is thin, and then names the exact thing that was missing and sends you back to the page. It marks by rules, so you can check the mark yourself — which is the only kind of practice that is worth doing twice.

How you know it worked

You will know it worked when you can say, out loud and calmly, “I don't know that, and here is what I would check first.”
